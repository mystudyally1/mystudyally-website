The paper SAT is gone. Since the switch to the digital format, the test is taken
on a laptop or tablet through the College Board's Bluebook app, runs just over
two hours instead of three, and adapts to how your child performs as they go. If
your reference point is the exam you or an older sibling sat, most of what you
remember about pacing and strategy no longer applies.

Here's what actually changed, and what it means for how your child should
prepare.

## The three changes that matter

**It's shorter.** Two sections — Reading & Writing, then Math — at roughly 2
hours 14 minutes total. Reading passages are now short single paragraphs with one
question each, so the old skill of managing a 700-word passage under time
pressure has been replaced by rapid context-switching across dozens of small
ones.

**It's adaptive.** Each section comes in two modules. How your child performs on
the first module determines the difficulty — and the score ceiling — of the
second. Early questions carry real weight, which changes pacing strategy:
accuracy up front matters more than saving time for the end.

**Calculators are always allowed.** The entire Math section permits a
calculator, and Bluebook has Desmos built in. Fluency with a graphing calculator
is now a scoreable skill, not a crutch.

## How prep should change

Practice materials written for the paper test now train the wrong instincts. Prep
should happen inside Bluebook's official practice tests — there are several full
adaptive tests available free — so the pacing, interface, and question style are
familiar on test day. For most students the highest-leverage work is a diagnostic
first, then targeted practice on the specific question types the diagnostic
exposes, rather than volume for its own sake.

A realistic timeline: one diagnostic three to four months out, focused work in
the weak areas, and full timed practice tests every two to three weeks in the
final stretch.

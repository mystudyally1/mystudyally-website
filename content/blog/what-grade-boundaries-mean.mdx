Every summer, someone points out that a grade 7 needed 62% one year and 58% the
next, and asks whether the exam got easier. It is a fair question with a
reassuring answer: the boundaries moved precisely so that the grade kept meaning
the same thing.

## What a boundary actually is

A grade boundary is not a standard set in advance. It is set *after* the papers
are marked, once examiners can see how the cohort performed on that specific
paper.

The purpose is comparability. A grade 7 should represent the same level of
achievement whether your child sat the paper this year or three years ago. Since
no two papers are exactly equal in difficulty, the mark required has to move to
keep the grade fixed.

So a lower boundary does not mean a easier exam or a weaker year. Usually it
means the opposite: the paper was harder than intended, and the boundary dropped
to compensate.

## Why they move

Three things drive the shift:

- **Paper difficulty.** The main factor. A paper with an unusually demanding
  question set produces lower raw marks across the board, so boundaries fall.
- **Cohort performance.** Awarding bodies compare this year's cohort with
  previous ones using prior attainment data, so a stronger or weaker year group is
  accounted for rather than rewarded or punished.
- **Tier and component.** Boundaries are set per paper and per tier. Foundation
  and Higher in the same subject move independently.

## How to read a mock result

This is where boundaries matter most for parents, and where they are most often
misread.

Schools usually mark mocks against a **previous year's** boundaries, because this
year's do not exist yet. That makes a mock grade an estimate built on a different
paper's standard — useful, but not a prediction.

Two practical consequences:

**Look at the mark, not just the grade.** "58, which was a 6 last summer" tells
you far more than "a 6". If the boundary for a 7 was 62, your child is four marks
away, and four marks is a conversation about where those marks were lost.

**Ask which paper the marks came from.** A student can be comfortably above the
boundary on Paper 1 and well below on Paper 2, and the averaged grade hides
exactly the information that would help.

## What this means for revision

Because boundaries respond to difficulty, chasing a percentage is less useful
than chasing specific marks. Ask the school — or a tutor — which questions the
marks were lost on and why: unfinished, misread, method missing, or genuinely not
known. Those four causes need four different responses, and only one of them is
about learning more content.
